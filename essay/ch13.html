Chapter 13 · anecdote · Aug 16

Sixty, Above a Glacier

I turned sixty on a Sunday, in the lobby of a hotel outside Denali National Park, waiting for a man to drive me to an airplane that was going to land on a glacier. That was the plan, anyway, and by this point in the book you know roughly what to expect from the plans.

The morning looked reasonable. There were clouds off toward the mountain, but there are always clouds off toward the mountain — Denali is big enough to make its own weather and mostly keeps the results to itself. We were cautiously optimistic. Eight of us loaded up for the flight, plus the pilot.

The pilot was Colin, and I want to tell you about Colin, because he was the best unintentional comedian we met in eleven days of Alaska.

Colin opened, before we ever left the ground, by way of introduction: he lived in Anchorage, he had friends and family visiting, he had been entertaining them late into the night, he had driven up that morning, and he was very tired. He shared all of this cheerfully, as a pilot might mention the weather or the cruising altitude — just context, really, for the day we were about to spend together in a small aircraft.

Then came the safety briefing. Colin asked whether anyone ever got airsick. One of the eight allowed that he sometimes did, in rough air. Colin reassured him warmly: not to worry, it happens, he himself got airsick on occasion.

Which is something you want to hear from your pilot.

We took off, and the air was rough — bouncing-around rough, especially early on and again near the end, and most especially through a gap in the mountains called Windy Pass, or possibly Wendy Pass; the plane was loud and I never settled the question. Either name works. If it's Windy, it was honestly labeled. If it's Wendy, then somewhere there is a woman who earned that.

Here is the part of small-plane flying in Alaska that nobody tells you about in advance. These flights operate under what's called VFR — visual flight rules — which means, in practice, that the way you avoid hitting other aircraft is that everyone looks out the window. The plane had weather radar. It did not, as far as I could tell, have anything that showed where the other planes were. That job belonged to eyeballs.

And there are other planes. Alaska has more pilots per capita than any state in the union — about one in every hundred Alaskans is licensed to fly, six times the national rate. The sky up there is not empty; it's a commute. And because mountains are mountains, all of that traffic funnels through the same handful of gaps — the same passes the highway uses, and the railroad, and us. The sky has a highway, and it follows the highway. So eight passengers spent a fair portion of the flight doing exactly what the arrangement quietly asks of you, which is watching for oncoming traffic, at altitude, in the weather.

The weather. Yes.

We flew toward the glacier, and every time we got near a place where Colin could put the plane down, the clouds got there first. So he'd bank away and try another approach, and the clouds would roll into that one too, patient as tide. This happened more than once. It happened enough times that the pattern became familiar: hope, approach, gray, turn.

Somewhere in the middle of all that came the moment I will be replaying for the rest of my life. We came through a patch of murk, and Colin — brightly, sincerely, with real relief in his voice — said:

"Yay, there is that ridge. I'm glad we found that."

I want to be fair to Colin, who flies these mountains for a living and knew precisely where that ridge was at all times. But there were stretches of that flight when the rock outside the right-hand window looked close enough to read, and I found myself weighing two options. Option one: yell "there is a mountain really close on the right side," a fact of which the pilot was presumably aware. Option two: stay calm and trust that Colin had the situation well in hand. I chose calm. I chose, to be precise, risking death over the mild embarrassment of overreacting, which I suspect is how most people my age go, and I stand by it.

In the end the fog and the rain won. There was no landing. Every route to the ice closed as we reached it, and Colin turned us for home, and on the way he gave the ruling that settles the matter as far as I'm concerned:

"It's probably not a great idea to try and land on something you can't quite see."

I have spent a career around people paid to state the obvious with authority. Colin did it for free, at altitude, and he was right.

So: I'm disappointed. I want to be honest about that and not dress it up. This was the centerpiece — sixty years old, standing on the Ruth Glacier — and instead I turned sixty in a bouncing plane, watching clouds close doors one by one. Bob and I had this day planned to the minute for months. Nobody plans the middle of the Alaska Range.

But I'm just as sure the decision was right, and I'd fly with Colin again tomorrow — tired, airsick-prone, magnificent Colin, who kept eight strangers laughing through weather that was genuinely trying to kill the itinerary, got us close enough for photographs I'll keep forever, and then declined, correctly, to gamble on a runway made of ice that he couldn't quite see.

And there's a symmetry to it that I didn't appreciate until the flight home. Two days earlier, on an ordinary Friday, from the window of a slow train, we had seen Denali entire — cloudless, complete, the whole impossible bulk of it, the view most visitors never get. Then on the day that was supposed to matter, the mountain drew the curtains. It shows itself on its own schedule, which has nothing to do with birthdays, and I find I respect it more for that, not less.

I turned sixty somewhere over the Alaska Range, between a ridge Colin was glad to find and a glacier I never touched. The glacier keeps. We have an anniversary next year.

Ledger for the day: glacier landings, none. Approaches attempted, several. Ridges located, one, to audible relief. Pilots airsick, zero, narrowly. Photographs, plenty. Regrets, fewer than you'd think.
