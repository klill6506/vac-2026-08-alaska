Chapter 16 · history · Aug 18

Four Minutes in 1964

We drove down to Seward on the last full day of the trip, three hours along the coast from Anchorage, to get on a boat and look at fjords. It is a very pretty town on a very pretty bay, working boats and a harbour and mountains coming straight down to the water.

Almost none of what you see there is older than 1964, and the reason is that on Good Friday of that year the ground moved for four and a half minutes and the waterfront went into the sea.

At thirty-six minutes past five in the afternoon on the twenty-seventh of March, 1964, a magnitude 9.2 earthquake occurred beneath Prince William Sound. It is the most powerful earthquake ever recorded in North America and the second most powerful ever recorded anywhere.

The number 9.2 does not communicate much on its own, so here is the thing that does: it lasted about four and a half minutes.

Sit somewhere and count that out. Most damaging earthquakes are over in thirty or forty seconds. This one went on long enough that people in Anchorage had time to leave buildings, watch the buildings come apart, and still be standing in the open while it continued.

What was happening underneath was the Pacific plate driving beneath the North American plate and, in one motion, releasing centuries of accumulated strain. The land itself was rearranged. Parts of the continental shelf rose by more than thirty feet — near Montague Island the uplift reached something like thirty-eight feet, lifting docks and seafloor permanently above the tideline. Elsewhere, around Kodiak and parts of the Kenai Peninsula, the land dropped by as much as eight feet, and towns that had been comfortably above the water found their streets flooding at high tide from then on.

Not damaged. Not rebuilt. Relocated, vertically, by a geological event lasting less time than it takes to boil a kettle.

Seward had a particular kind of bad luck.

The town sits at the head of Resurrection Bay on ground that turned out to be loose sediment. When the shaking started, roughly a kilometre of the Seward waterfront simply slid off into the bay — docks, warehouses, the railroad terminal, all of it sliding underwater and generating a local tsunami that came back at the town within minutes, well before the main wave from the sound arrived.

And in the middle of that were the oil tanks. They ruptured and caught fire, and the fuel went out onto the water and kept burning, so that the wave that came back into Seward was carrying fire on top of it.

Between the local slide-generated wave and the regional tsunami that followed, somewhere in the region of eleven to thirteen people were killed in Seward. The earthquake and its tsunamis killed 131 people in all — a figure that is astonishingly low for an event of that size, and which owes a great deal to the timing. It was a holiday evening. The schools were empty. The downtown business districts had largely closed.

Four minutes after the shaking stopped, a wave destroyed the village of Chenega.

The southern terminus of the Alaska Railroad — the railroad from the last chapter, the one that took nine years and twice its budget and built a city as a byproduct — is Seward. Its dock and terminal were among the things that went into Resurrection Bay that evening.

The town was rebuilt. Of course it was. That's what the boat harbour is, and the shops, and the office where we checked in for a six-hour cruise in the rain with a deli sandwich each. There is a memorial and there are photographs in various places, and if you did not go looking you would spend the day there without ever quite registering that the ground you parked on is younger than you are.

I have been thinking about a remark I made earlier in this book — that standing in Denali, looking at country with nothing manmade in it, was unlike any room I've been in. That was true. But it was incomplete, and Seward is the correction.

The emptiness up in the park is stable. Nothing has happened there for a long time and nothing much is going to. The country around Prince William Sound is not stable at all. It is the actively moving edge of two plates, and the last time it moved in earnest it lifted a shoreline thirty-eight feet, dropped another one eight, set fire to the sea and rewrote every chart of the coast.

We spent our last full day on that water, watching glaciers calve into it and sea otters float about on their backs, and the whole arrangement felt permanent and serene and had been substantially redrawn within living memory.

Sources: U.S. Geological Survey and NOAA accounts of the 1964 Great Alaska Earthquake; Alaska Earthquake Center; contemporary and retrospective reporting on Seward and Chenega.
