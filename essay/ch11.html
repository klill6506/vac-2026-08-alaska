Chapter 11 · anecdote · Aug 15

The Absence of Squirrels

Our bus into Denali was postponed on the morning of the tour, from one o'clock to half past three, and by that stage of the holiday this did not register as news. It was the third time an Alaskan outfit had moved an appointment on us in five days. I had stopped experiencing these as disruptions and started experiencing them as weather.

Bob had flagged it days earlier — check the departure time two days out, the operator may adjust it. I read the note, agreed with it, and did not do it. There is a specific annoyance in being let down by yourself when you have already been advised in writing.

Half past three, then, which everyone assured me was the better slot. More angled light. Animals more active in the cool of the evening. I had been told this with some confidence, including by me.

The Denali Park Road is ninety-two miles long, one lane wide, and gravel for most of its length, and it is the only road there is. Six million acres of country — larger than New Hampshire — and one thin gravel line laid into it. Private vehicles are not allowed past a certain point. Essentially everyone who goes into Denali goes in on a bus, on that road, looking out of a window.

Ours went in as far as Mile 43 and turned around, for reasons that are so interesting they get a chapter of their own.

The scenery was enormous. Braided rivers going silver in the low sun. Tundra running up into mountains with nothing on it, no fences, no buildings, no wires, nothing at all that a person had put there. It is very difficult to describe emptiness in a way that sounds like a compliment, but I have been in a lot of rooms in my life and almost none of them had that.

And then, because I am who I am, I began keeping a tally.

Bears: one, at a distance sufficient that it might have been a stump with opinions. Moose: none. Caribou: several, which was genuinely good. Dall sheep: some, high up, white specks doing whatever it is they do up there.

That was the day's take, across five and a half hours in one of the great wildlife parks on earth, and I record it without complaint. Wild animals are not obliged to be anywhere. But I had come off a floatplane four days earlier having watched twelve brown bears fish a creek from fifteen feet away, and there is no recovering from that. The bar had been set by professionals.

What I did not expect — what has stayed with me far longer than the tally — was something I noticed only slowly, over about three hours of looking out of a window at one of the emptiest places in North America.

There were no squirrels.

I do not mean there were no interesting animals. I mean there was none of the ordinary background traffic of the living world. At home in Georgia, if I stand in my own yard for ninety seconds I will be visited by squirrels — plural, argumentative — and cardinals and jays and mockingbirds and a woodpecker working on something, and a crow will say something rude from a wire. This is not nature. This is just what outdoors sounds like. It is the wallpaper.

Denali has no wallpaper. Hour after hour of country going by, and a squirrel would have been an event. A crow would have been an event. I would have taken a raven and been grateful, and I never in my life expected to write the phrase I would have taken a raven.

It unsettled me more than the bear did, and it took me until we were home to work out why.

The numbers, once I went looking, are startling. Those six million acres hold about thirty-nine species of mammal. One hundred and sixty-nine species of bird. And — this is the figure that stopped me — one amphibian. One. In an area the size of a small country, there is a single kind of frog. In Georgia I could probably manage a dozen without leaving the county.

That is not Denali failing at something. It is simply what the subarctic is. Diversity like that is normal for the far north, and the reason is the calendar. The growing season is a rumor. Almost everything that can leave, does: roughly eighty percent of the park's birds are migratory, arriving in May and gone again by September. We were there in the third week of August, which means we had arrived just in time for the great annual departure — the birds were not hiding from us, they were checking out. The wallpaper was being rolled up for the season while we watched.

So the emptiness I had been admiring all afternoon as scenery turned out to be literal. It was not that the animals were elsewhere in the park. There are, in the round, simply far fewer animals — fewer kinds, fewer of each — because the place can only support life for a few months at a stretch and everything living there is either leaving, sleeping, or built for it.

I have thought about this a good deal since. We fly to Alaska to be impressed by the enormous — the bears, the mountain, the ice — and the thing that actually rearranged something in my head was an absence I would never have thought to look for. At home, a squirrel is noise. It is the least remarkable event available. Up there, the ordinary is the rare part, and you only notice once you have spent five hours failing to see any of it.

Georgianna, when I explained all this to her at some length on the bus, said that it was very peaceful.

We reached Mile 43 in the long yellow light everyone had promised me, and the light was in fact excellent, and the animals were not notably more active, and the driver turned us around and took us back the way we came. We got in around nine at night. I ate something. I have no memory of what.

The one thing I did do, before anything else, was take the paper receipt from the tour and put it in my wallet — not in a bag, not in a pocket, in the wallet — because that flimsy little slip was also my National Park entrance pass, and I needed to show it to a pilot the following morning.

The following morning being my sixtieth birthday, and the pilot being the man who was going to land a plane on a glacier.

Ledger to date: Bears, one, distant. Moose, none. Caribou, several. Dall sheep, some. Squirrels, none. Crows, none. Ravens, none — and I looked. Amphibians available parkwide: one species. Reminders issued by Bob: one. Reminders acted upon: none.

Sources for the figures: National Park Service, Denali National Park & Preserve — wildlife and bird migration.
