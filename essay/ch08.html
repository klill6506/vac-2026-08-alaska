Chapter 8 · history · Aug 13

The Glacier That Invoices the Town

On the Monday we admired the Mendenhall Glacier from a boat. On the Tuesday we walked up to its face at Nugget Falls. On the Wednesday we landed on top of it in a helicopter and rode a dog sled across it.

At no point during any of that did it occur to me that the glacier was, over those same three days, in the process of emptying several billion gallons of water into the neighbourhoods below.

It had been filling all summer. We simply happened to be visiting during the week the bill came due.

Here is what is actually going on up there, and it is worth understanding, because it is one of the more elegant pieces of destructive plumbing in North America.

Alongside the Mendenhall Glacier sits a side basin. It was carved by a tributary glacier that has since retreated, leaving behind a deep rock bowl with the main glacier still pressed against one side of it. The bowl has a name that nobody chose for its cheerfulness: Suicide Basin.

Every summer that basin fills — snowmelt, glacier melt, rain, and Juneau supplies a great deal of rain. The Mendenhall Glacier itself forms the dam. Ice is a perfectly adequate dam right up until the moment it isn't, and what determines that moment is not the height of the water but the pressure at the bottom of it.

As the basin fills, that pressure starts to force its way into small flaws in the ice. Water begins to trickle underneath the glacier. And because the water is warmer than the ice, every trickle melts its own channel slightly wider, which admits more water, which melts it wider still. The process feeds itself, slowly at first and then not slowly at all.

Eventually the pressure becomes sufficient to do something that sounds impossible when you say it out loud, and which I have now read in several sober government documents: it lifts the glacier. The whole enormous weight of the thing floats, just enough, just briefly, and the basin drains out underneath it through subglacial channels into Mendenhall Lake, and from there into the Mendenhall River, and from there into a valley full of houses.

Estimates run to something like fifteen billion gallons in a single release.

Icelanders, who have been dealing with this sort of thing for a very long time, have a word for it: jökulhlaup. Glacier leap. Which is a considerably better name than anything we have come up with, and exactly describes what the water does.

The first recorded outburst from Suicide Basin was in 2011.

That is the detail I keep turning over. Not 1911. Not some centuries-old seasonal fact of Juneau life that the town has adapted to over generations. Twenty eleven. Within the working lifetime of most of the people it now threatens.

Since then it has happened every year, sometimes more than once a year, and the floods have been getting worse. The 2023 release was described at the time as the worst glacial outburst flood in Juneau's history. The 2024 flood beat it, cresting just under sixteen feet on the river gauge. In August 2025 the river passed that record too, cresting at 16.65 feet.

And in August 2026, while two visitors from Georgia went whale watching and walked to a waterfall and rode a dog sled on the roof of the responsible party, the National Weather Service was forecasting a crest of between 16.3 and 16.8 feet, and the City and Borough of Juneau was advising something in the region of nineteen hundred homes to evacuate.

The Army Corps of Engineers had spent the year installing temporary flood barriers along the river. The U.S. Geological Survey watches the basin with cameras and a laser, measuring water and ice levels so the Weather Service can forecast what's coming and when. There is an entire apparatus of federal attention pointed at one bowl of water above one Alaskan town, because the bowl now empties itself annually and nobody can yet tell it not to.

What I find difficult to hold in my head is the ordinariness of it.

The Mendenhall Glacier is a tourist attraction. There is a visitor centre with a gift shop. There is a paved trail out to Nugget Falls, and we walked it on a pleasant Tuesday morning along with a couple of hundred other people in sensible jackets, and the glacier sat there being photogenic and enormous and entirely still, the way glaciers do.

Meanwhile, a few hundred feet above and behind all that, a rock bowl was filling toward the point at which it would pick up the entire glacier and post the contents down the valley.

Both of those things are the Mendenhall. The postcard and the invoice. And the people who live along that river have had to learn, over about fifteen years, that the scenery in their back garden has taken up an annual habit — one that did not exist when most of them bought their houses, and which gets a little more expensive every August.

We flew out of Juneau on the Thursday evening, over the valley, as the water was still coming up. From the air it looked like a river doing what rivers do.
