Chapter 9 · anecdote · Aug 14

Seven Swans, Not A-Swimming

The Alaska Railroad runs a glass-domed observation car called the GoldStar, and I would like to begin by saying that everything about it was excellent. This is not throat-clearing before a complaint. It is the point.

The ticketing was smooth. The luggage went where luggage is supposed to go and reappeared where it was supposed to reappear, twice, without incident. The food was genuinely delicious. The drinks were fine and there were two of them included, which is two more than most forms of transport manage. And the staff — every last one of them — were so friendly and so helpful that I began, after a few hours, to find it slightly suspicious.

The train left Anchorage at twenty past eight in the morning and reached Denali at twenty to four in the afternoon. Seven hours and twenty minutes.

I want to be careful here, because I am about to say something ungrateful about one of the great railway journeys in North America, and the people who run it deserve better than that. So: the railroad was superb, and seven and a half hours is a very long time to look at trees.

I love trees. I want that on the record too. I could not live in southern California or Utah or any of those handsome bare western states, precisely because of the shortage of trees. Trees are, in my view, one of the finer things a landscape can do with itself.

You can, however, get too much of a good thing.

The country between Anchorage and Denali is spruce, and then more spruce, and then a small town, and then spruce. It is beautiful for the first hour and lovely for the second and by the fourth you have begun to look forward to the small towns in a way that would embarrass you if you described it out loud.

And the wildlife, from the window of that train, over seven and a half hours, came to a total of seven swans.

Seven. I counted them, obviously. And I regret to report that they were not a-swimming. They were standing about on the ground, doing nothing whatsoever, in the manner of swans who have not been told what is expected of them at this point in the song.

There was one thing, though, and it justified the entire day.

Somewhere in the middle of all that spruce, the clouds came off the mountain and Denali stood out clear — the whole enormous white bulk of it, cloudless, from base to summit.

I did not appreciate at the time how unusual that was. Denali is high enough and heavy enough to manufacture its own weather, and it spends most of the summer wrapped in the results. Plenty of people come to this park for a week and never see the mountain at all. We got it entire, on a Friday, through a train window, on an ordinary travel day when we had asked for nothing.

Bear that in mind. It comes up again.

That evening we went to Cabin Nite, which is a dinner theatre at Denali Park Village, and which I approached with a specific and well-founded dread.

I get chosen. This has been true my whole life. Magicians find me. Comedians find me. If there is a moment in a show when a performer scans a darkened room for somebody to bring up on stage, that performer and I will make eye contact, and it will be settled between us instantly and without discussion.

So I arrived braced. And then Georgianna — my wife of twenty-nine years, the woman who has spotted every bear on this trip before I did — informed the staff that it was my birthday.

She handed them the ammunition. She walked into a room full of people whose job is to select an audience member for public participation, and she volunteered that the gentleman at that table was turning sixty.

They spared me. To this day I don't know why. The show was exactly what we expected and rather less corny than I'd feared, the ribs and mashed potatoes and corn and biscuits and cobbler were the kind of thing my mother used to put on a table, and I was not once required to stand up in front of anybody.

I would tell you more about it, but there isn't more. It was a pleasant evening, competently done, and nothing about it was remarkable — which, on a day that had already produced a cloudless Denali and seven idle swans, was perhaps all it needed to be.

Although I will note, since we are being thorough, that two Georgians who flew three thousand miles to escape Georgia were served ribs, corn, biscuits and cobbler in a hall in the Alaskan interior. The state had followed us north and was now doing the catering.

We rode the same railroad back on the Monday, southbound, and it was a carbon copy: same helpfulness, same excellent food, same faultless business with the luggage, same trees.

The same trees for seven and a half hours in the other direction, which I had signed up for voluntarily, in advance, with full knowledge.

But on that return leg, off in the distance and lying on the ground, we finally saw a moose.

I had given up. I had privately closed the file on moose. We had been to Denali National Park — six million acres, moose everywhere in the literature — and had come away without one, and I had made my peace with the fact that this was going to be the trip where the moose did not appear.

He was a long way off. He was lying down, doing nothing, which by now I understood to be standard practice for Alaskan wildlife encountered from a railway carriage. But he was unmistakably, distinctly a moose, and I have rarely been so pleased to see an animal decline to do anything at all.

There is one more thing about those two train days, and it isn't about trains.

We are Southerners. We were raised on southern hospitality, which we are accustomed to regarding as the gold standard — the thing other regions attempt and don't quite manage.

I am obliged to report that the South has nothing on Alaska.

Not one person, in eleven days. The waiter at our hotel restaurant in Denali, who as we were leaving urged us to take a danish and a banana from the buffet for later, and then brought us coffee in cups to go. The waiter at a pizza place in Denali who was so pleasant we talked about him afterwards. The shuttle driver who, entirely unprompted, offered to drop us at the park visitor centre instead of the hotel so that we wouldn't have to catch a second shuttle — and then offered to run our fellow passengers to dinner in the next town that evening and collect them afterwards. Nobody asked him. It hadn't occurred to anybody that this was a thing that could be asked.

And once I noticed it, I couldn't stop noticing it backwards through the whole trip. Two men named Dan running a whale boat as though we were the first people ever to see a humpback. A whale company refunding money we had not requested and did not know we were owed. A guide who put herself between six strangers and a brown bear without any apparent thought about it. A hundred sled dogs kept by people who knew every one of their names. A tired pilot who kept eight of us laughing through weather that beat him.

Seven and a half hours of trees, twice, and the thing I'll actually remember from the Alaska Railroad is that everybody who worked on it was kind to us for no reason at all.

Ledger for the rails: hours aboard, roughly fifteen, round trip. Swans, seven, stationary. Moose, one, eventually, also stationary. Mountains fully revealed, one, on the day we weren't asking. Members of staff who were anything less than delightful: none.
