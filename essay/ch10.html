Chapter 10 · history · Aug 14

Four Hundred and Seventy Miles of Nothing

I have just spent a chapter complaining about trees, so it seems only decent to explain what it took to put me among them.

The Alaska Railroad runs 470 miles, from the port of Seward on the southern coast, up through Anchorage, past Denali, and on to Fairbanks in the interior. When the United States government decided to build it, there was no Anchorage. That is not a figure of speech. The largest city in Alaska exists because somebody needed somewhere to put the construction camp.

Work began in 1914. The reasoning was coal, mostly — there were mineral deposits in the interior of Alaska and no way to get them to a port, and private capital had looked at the problem and declined it with some firmness. So the federal government did something it had not done before and has rarely done since: it built and owned a railroad outright.

The estimate was thirty-five million dollars.

The final figure was close to double that, which I mention not to be smug about a hundred-year-old cost overrun but because it is the single most honest detail in the whole story. Everyone involved understood the terrain in theory. Nobody had built through it.

In 1915 the government bought seventy miles of existing track from a defunct private line and established a headquarters and construction camp on the mud flats of Cook Inlet. A tent city went up more or less overnight as thousands of men came north for the wages. That camp is Anchorage. We slept in it twice, in hotels, eleven decades later, and I had no idea until I went looking that the city began as an administrative convenience.

At its peak, in 1917, the railroad had more than 4,500 men working on it.

Consider what that work actually was. Four hundred and seventy miles of subarctic country — muskeg that swallows equipment, rivers that reroute themselves annually, permafrost that behaves like rock until you disturb it and then behaves like soup, and a construction season that closes for winter with no interest in your schedule. There were no roads. Everything came in by ship and then by the railroad you were in the middle of building, which is a logistical arrangement that only works if you never stop.

The principal engineer was a U.S. Army colonel named Frederick Mears. He was transferred to Seattle four months before the line was finished, which strikes me as the kind of thing that happens to the person who does the work — a theme this book has run into before.

On the fifteenth of July, 1923, President Warren G. Harding stood near Nenana and drove a golden spike to mark the completion of the line. Accounts agree that he tapped it twice, lightly, then had it removed and replaced with an ordinary spike, which he then drove properly into the final coupling.

I like that detail more than I can justify. The ceremonial spike gets tapped for the photograph and taken back out. The real one gets hammered in and stays. Somewhere in that is the entire relationship between how things are commemorated and how they are actually finished.

Harding was dead eighteen days later.

He had come north on what was billed as a "Voyage of Understanding," became unwell shortly afterward, and died in San Francisco on the second of August, 1923. The last great ceremonial act of his presidency was completing a railroad through the Alaskan interior, and he did not live long enough to see the photographs published.

So: seven and a half hours, twice, and I complained about the trees.

I want to be careful how I put this, because I stand by the complaint — fifteen hours is a long time in a chair, and there is a great deal of spruce between Anchorage and Denali, and no amount of history makes hour five pass faster.

But it is worth knowing, while you sit in a glass-domed carriage being handed a complimentary drink by somebody who is genuinely pleased to see you, that you're travelling on a nine-year federal construction project that ran to roughly twice its budget, employed four and a half thousand men at its peak, invented a city as a side effect, and was finished by a president with less than three weeks to live.

The spruce is still monotonous. I simply have a better sense now of what it cost to be bored by it in comfort.

And there is one more thing I did not appreciate at the time. Our whole trip was hung on that line without my noticing. We flew into Anchorage — a railroad camp. We rode the train north to Denali and back. And on the last full day we drove down to Seward, which is not a random fishing town on the Kenai Peninsula but the southern end of the same railroad, the seaport the whole thing was built to reach.

Bob and I had planned eleven days across southern Alaska. It turns out we had mostly planned a tour of a hundred-year-old government infrastructure project, which is either a coincidence or a demonstration that in a state this size, the few things anybody ever managed to build determine where everybody goes.
