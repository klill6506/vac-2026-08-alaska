Chapter 3 · anecdote · Aug 11

Fifteen Feet

The most expensive single day of our vacation began in the parking lot behind a gym. I had wired Above & Beyond Alaska a genuinely startling amount of money for the privilege of standing on the far side of a wilderness so remote it has no bathrooms, no shelter, no dock, and no cell service — and the appointed gateway to all this primal grandeur was the back lot of Pavitts Health & Fitness, Auke Bay, where we waited beside our rental Volvo while, through the windows, perfectly ordinary people did squats. There is probably a lesson in that. I have decided not to look for it.

Except that nobody came. It developed, in due course, that the meeting point had been moved to another spot entirely, a fact the company had not judged worth mentioning to the people it was meeting. My working theory is that the gym had finally objected to serving as the departure lounge for a wilderness airline, which I understand — you install mirrors and a smoothie bar and one day your parking lot is full of people in head-to-toe rain gear looking expectantly at the sky. And so the day's first tracking exercise, before any bear was involved, was locating our own tour.

They had already moved our departure once — from 7:30 in the morning to 1:30 in the afternoon, announced by text five days before the trip, in the casual tone of a dentist's office bumping a cleaning. First the time, now the place: this was an outfit with a relaxed relationship to fixed coordinates. Bob and I had run the implications the way we run everything, and they were not wonderful: the new slot meant that if weather scrubbed the flight there was no day left to make it up, and that we would be dining, if at all, at whatever Juneau establishment had failed to lock its doors by 8:30. But there is no arguing with a text message, and anyway the afternoon had the last word, as you'll see.

The airplane was a floatplane, my first. I had always assumed floatplanes were one of those things, like oysters and jury duty, that people describe as "fine once you're in it," and this turned out to be exactly right. It was neat — I can offer no more sophisticated word. You climb in off a dock, the water lets go of you at some point you can't precisely identify, and suddenly the whole of Southeast Alaska is arranged below like a scale model that got out of hand.

And here is the thing nobody tells you about the flight to a bear tour: it is also the best whale watching in Juneau. From a few hundred feet up we could see humpbacks scattered across the channels below us — more than we'd seen from the boat the day before — and as we watched, two of them breached, fully, clearing the water in that impossible way that looks fake even when it is happening directly beneath you. I had paid good money to watch whales from a boat on Monday. The superior whale watching came free on Tuesday, as a side effect of going to look at something else. I have been in accounting for thirty-three years and I am telling you there is no line item for this.

We landed on water near Chichagof Island, which holds more brown bears per square mile than anywhere else on earth — a statistic I had found thrilling at booking time and was now, on final approach, reconsidering from several angles.

Our guide was Kelsey. I liked her immediately, partly for her competence and partly because she talked about "Yachties" — her term, delivered with the weary precision of a field biologist classifying a lesser species — meaning the people who anchor their yachts off these islands and go tromping into bear country in boat shoes, without a guide, without spray, without a plan, apparently on the theory that money is repellent. Kelsey did not think much of Yachties. We, by contrast, had paid to be led in properly, which I felt gave us a certain moral standing, right up until the walk began.

Because the most dangerous part of a brown bear tour is not the brown bears. It is the walk to the brown bears — a slog over mud with ambitions, wet boulders, and tree roots laid across the trail like exposed wiring, every third step of it punctuated by enormous piles of bear scat in various states of freshness, each one a small informational plaque reading YOU ARE IN IT NOW. You proceed through this at the careful, flat-footed shuffle of a man who has already imagined the incident report.

And then the creek — where two bears were already stationed, waiting, as though they'd had us in the reservation book. We had flown thirty minutes and hiked through all that mud to see bears, and the bears had arranged to be early. It is the only appointment in Alaska that beat us to the venue.

We saw ten, maybe twelve different bears that afternoon — the count drifts because they came and went like commuters — and the single most striking thing about them was how comprehensively they did not care about us. Mostly they worked the stream at fifty to a hundred yards, which sounds like a comfortable distance until you register the scale of the things. "They are really big" is what my brain kept submitting, and I have decided to let the assessment stand, because it is accurate and nothing fancier is. And they were silent. That was the part no documentary had prepared me for — animals the size of chest freezers moving through water and deadfall without a sound, all afternoon. Even their disputes were silent. When one bear drifted too close to another's pool there was no roaring, no swatting, none of the theater you've been promised; the two of them would simply look at each other, steadily, for as long as it took, until one of them decided the fishing wasn't worth the conversation and walked away. I have sat in partner meetings conducted on exactly this principle.

The salmon, meanwhile, were running — hurling themselves out of the water in a continuous silver sputter, the stream delivering the buffet directly to the diners — and six humans in borrowed rain gear ranked somewhere below the weather as a subject of bear interest. There is something clarifying about standing near an apex predator and understanding, at a cellular level, that you are not even the second most interesting thing in its field of view.

Which held true right up until one of them came toward us.

Fifteen feet, maybe twenty. I want to be honest about what that distance means: it is the length of a modest living room. It is a putt I would expect to miss. The received wisdom is that you must never run from a grizzly, and I can now report that I came very close to testing that theory on everyone's behalf. What actually happened is that Kelsey — unphased in a way I found frankly supernatural — moved down and crouched between our party and the bear, spray in hand, calm as a woman waiting for a bus, while behind her six adults performed the act of standing still the way you perform it when it is the only item on the menu. The bear considered us. We were considered. And then it turned, the way bears apparently do when the salmon are this good, and wandered off downstream to resume eating its body weight, and the six of us began breathing again in ragged, unsynchronized relief.

Georgianna, I should record, said nothing quotable during any of this, which is itself worth reporting, because she is the talker of our operation — the one who has befriended half a floatplane before I've finished reading the safety card. Out on the creek, all that sociability converted into something more useful: she became our early-warning system. Every time a new bear stepped out of the trees — every time, all afternoon — it was Georgianna who saw it first and said so, quietly, before any of the rest of us had noticed anything but salmon. Twenty-nine years of marriage and the woman can still produce a brand-new skill on a Tuesday.

Kelsey straightened up as though she had done nothing more than retrieve a dropped glove. I made a mental note to revisit her gratuity, upward, in cash — cash being the only instrument accepted at an establishment with no doors — and I can confirm the revision was executed.

Ledger to date: Bears, twelve. Bathrooms, none. Whales, more from the airplane than from the boat. Gratuity revised upward to reflect one (1) interposed human body. Distance to nearest bear, fifteen feet. Faith in Kelsey: unpriceable, which is not a term we use in the profession.
