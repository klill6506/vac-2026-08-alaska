Chapter 5 · anecdote · Aug 12

A Hundred Dogs on an Icefield

There was a partial eclipse of the sun over Juneau that Wednesday morning. I had been briefed on it in some detail — the coverage, the timings, the fact that sunglasses are useless for looking at it and that the sensible approach is to punch a hole in a card and watch the shadow instead.

We did not notice it. Not a flicker. Sixteen percent of the sun went missing directly above our heads and neither of us registered a thing, which is exactly what I had been told would happen, and it remains the only celestial event I have ever been thoroughly prepared for and completely failed to observe.

The morning had also been earmarked for the Mount Roberts tramway, which turns out to have been closed for several weeks and stayed closed for several more. So we hiked the Perseverance Trail instead, out of the top of town, which is the old mining route up the valley where this whole city came from — and which I'd recommend over a tramway to anybody, though I concede I am arguing from a position of not having had the choice.

The dogs were at a quarter to three.

You reach the dog camp by helicopter, because it is on a glacier, and there is no other sensible way to arrive at a glacier. We had flown in helicopters before, so the novelty wasn't the aircraft. What I noticed instead was the difference between the two halves of the trip. Going out, it was transportation — steady, level, businesslike, a man taking eight people to work. Coming home, the pilot opened it up: faster, banking hard through the turns, the whole machine leaning into it. Transportation on the way up and a ride on the way back.

And then you step out onto the Mendenhall Glacier, which we had now approached by boat, on foot, and by air in the space of three days, and which was at that precise moment in the process of emptying several billion gallons of itself into the valley below. The town was under an evacuation advisory. We were standing on the roof of the thing that was causing it, waiting to meet some dogs. Nobody mentioned this. It seemed rude to bring up.

The scale of the camp surprised me. I had pictured a dozen dogs and a sled. There were teams running four trips at a time, and after all of that activity there were still well over a hundred dogs in camp — a whole working town of them out on the ice, in tidy rows, most of them asleep, some of them emphatically not.

Because here is the thing nobody prepares you for: they want to run. Not tolerate running. Not perform running for tourists. They want it the way a retriever wants a tennis ball thrown, except that there are forty of them and they have been bred for two centuries to want exactly this. There is no whipping. There is no cajoling, no dragging, none of the grim business the word "sled dog" might suggest to somebody who has only read about it. You tell them to go and they go, and the difficult part — the genuinely difficult part — is getting them to stop being enthusiastic before it is time to leave.

Georgianna sat in the basket and I stood on the runners, and then we swapped, which is how I came to be standing up on the back of a dog sled at fifty-nine years old, four days short of sixty, on top of a glacier, in short sleeves.

That last detail requires explanation.

It was in the high seventies. On an icefield. There was snow in every direction and a hard blue sky over it and the temperature was that of a pleasant afternoon in Athens, Georgia. Some of the camp staff were working in shorts and short sleeves. Actual shorts, on a glacier, in front of the ice.

We had come three thousand miles to get away from Georgia weather, and here it was, waiting for us on top of a glacier, being enjoyed by Alaskans dressed for my hometown.

The people who run that camp live there. Not nearby — there. Six days at a stretch on the ice, then one day down in the city, then back up. They do this for the season.

Most of them race. Some had already run the Iditarod, others were working toward it, and all of them talked about it the way people talk about a thing they have decided is worth arranging a life around. One of them mentioned, without any particular drama, that during his last race he had burned off a whole pants size.

I have spent my career converting effort into figures, and I want to say that this is the best unit of measurement I have ever encountered. Not calories. Not miles. A whole pants size — a number you can wear, arrived at by pulling a sled across Alaska in March.

But the thing I keep coming back to isn't the racing. It's that they knew all the dogs. Every one, by name, and not just the name — the personality, the quirks, who got on with whom. Over a hundred animals and these people could tell you which one sulked.

One of the dogs on our team had anxiety. That was how it was explained to us, plainly, the way you'd mention that a colleague doesn't like open-plan offices. And so we were invited to go and pet him, because it helped, and he was — and I want to be precise here, because Georgianna and I have discussed it since and we are in complete agreement — the cutest dog of the entire hundred.

They let us hold the puppies at the end. I am not going to attempt to be dry about the puppies.

The best part, though, and I did not expect to write this, was not the dogs.

It was where we were standing. The camp sits high on the icefield with mountains all round and nothing manmade in any direction except the small tidy settlement of tents and dogs and sleds we happened to be standing in, and the light coming off all that snow is like nothing available at sea level. You genuinely do feel as though you are on top of the world. I have no better way to put it and I have stopped trying to find one.

We flew back down — the good half, the banking half — and had dinner that night at Tracy's King Crab Shack on the waterfront, where I paid ninety-nine dollars for a single crab leg.

I want to be fair to the crab leg. It was good. It was not ninety-nine dollars good, but it was good, and I would like the record to reflect that I ate all of it and did not once complain out loud, which for me constitutes a rave review. While we sat there an enormous cruise ship pulled out of the harbour in front of us, thousands of people going somewhere else, and we watched it go with the particular smugness of people who are staying.

Down the valley, a few miles off, the river was rising toward the top of its banks and the neighbourhoods along it were emptying out. We would fly out over all of it the following evening.

Ledger for the day: eclipses observed, none, despite preparation. Trams ridden, none, closed. Dogs met, a hundred-odd. Dogs held, several, small. Anxious dogs comforted, one, the cutest. Pants sizes burned, one, by somebody else. Crab legs, one, ninety-nine dollars.
