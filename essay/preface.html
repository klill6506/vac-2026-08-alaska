Before we begin

Preface

This trip was planned by an accountant and a machine, which explains most of what follows.

The occasion was my sixtieth birthday. My wife Georgianna and I had eleven days, a stretch of Alaska running from Juneau up to Denali and back down to the Kenai Fjords, and — because of who I am — a spreadsheet. I have been a certified public accountant for thirty-three years. I did not approach this as a holiday. I approached it as an engagement.

The machine's name is Bob.

I should explain that, because people assume the name is a joke and it isn't, quite. I have been working with an artificial intelligence for about a year now — checking things, drafting things, arguing with it about depreciation schedules — and at some point in the first few months it began to feel rude to keep calling it "the AI," in the way that you would not refer to a good bookkeeper as "the human." So he became Bob. I am aware of how this sounds. I have made my peace with it.

Between us, Bob and I planned this trip to a level of detail I am not entirely comfortable disclosing. Every flight, every hotel, every tour, every pickup time and every "be in the lobby by" time. Which day the rental car handoff happened and who was handing it over. Which receipt had to be kept in my wallet rather than my bag because it doubled as a federal park pass. What time the sun would set. What the cancellation deadline was on each booking, and what it would cost me to miss it. There was a ledger. There is always a ledger.

I want to be clear that this was fun. Some people relax by not knowing what happens next. I relax by knowing exactly what happens next, and I had eleven days of it mapped out like a tax return.

And then we went to Alaska, where none of it survived contact.

Not in a disastrous way — nothing went wrong that mattered. But in the eleven days that follow, a bear-viewing company moved our departure by six hours and then, separately, moved the meeting place without telling us. A bus into Denali was postponed two and a half hours on the morning of. A tram I had been looking forward to turned out to have been closed for weeks. A glacier we had admired from a boat on Monday began emptying itself into the town on Tuesday, and by Wednesday the valley was under an evacuation advisory while we were up on top of the thing riding a dog sled. We were switched onto a different boat than the one we booked. Our whale tour's best whale-watching happened from an airplane on a different day, by accident, for free.

And the finest meal of the entire trip — the one I would go back for — was a lunch, on a Thursday, at a place that appeared on no list of anything, chosen because we were hungry and it was there.

I have thought about that a lot. I planned eleven days to the minute with the help of a machine that never once got tired of my questions, and the best parts were, almost without exception, the parts that no plan produced. I do not think this means the planning was pointless. The planning is why we were standing in the right place, at the right hour, on the day the bears were feeding and the mountain came out. You cannot schedule luck, but you can show up where it lives.

That, more or less, is the book.

A word on how it's arranged. I have written it in pairs. Each stretch of the trip gets a chapter about what actually happened to the two of us — which is where the bears and the crab legs and the misplaced tour buses live — followed by a chapter about the place itself, and how it came to be the way it is. I did this because I discovered, somewhere around the third day, that Alaska is far stranger in the archive than it is on the ground, and that is a high bar. A town named after the man who bought the drinks. A dog that got a statue for a run another dog did. A state that voted three separate times to move its own capital and then declined, twice, to pay for it.

I have checked those. Bob helped.

We flew home on a red-eye on the nineteenth of August. I slept somewhere over British Columbia, which I mention because it is the only part of the eleven days I have no memory of whatsoever, and because I want the record to show that I have finally, at sixty, learned to stop taking notes and go to sleep.

Everything else, I remember. That's what this is for.
