Chapter 1 · anecdote · Aug 9–10

Two Dans and a Demotion

We went to Alaska for the cool weather. I want to establish that at the outset, because it is the only part of the plan that failed completely, and I believe in getting the failures on the record early.

In Georgia, where we live, August is less a month than a punishment. The air reaches the low nineties by breakfast and then sits on you for the rest of the day like a large damp dog. So when we planned eleven days in Alaska, the brochure copy wrote itself: glaciers, fjords, whales, and air that has never once been described as "soupy." We packed fleece. We packed synthetic base layers, which I now know is what outdoor people call long underwear so they can charge more for it. We packed for the Alaska of calendars and cruise commercials.

We then arrived, with the timing that has quietly governed my whole life, at the start of the warmest week Alaska would produce all year — a state, I should note, that has responded to two centuries of this possibility by not installing air conditioning in anything. You cannot cool a room in Alaska. You can open a window, and the window will look at you, and that is the full extent of the negotiation.

It is worth putting a number on this. Ninety degrees in Georgia, indoors, with air conditioning, is a materially cooler experience than seventy-five degrees in Alaska without it. This is not a paradox and it is not a complaint; it is arithmetic, and I would be happy to walk anyone through it. Seventy-five degrees with nowhere to put the heat is simply a larger number than ninety degrees with somewhere to put it. We had flown three thousand miles to escape the heat of Georgia, and the heat, showing frankly more initiative than I'd given it credit for, had come with us.

The hotel provided a fan.

But I'm ahead of myself.

Getting to Juneau from Atlanta takes most of a day and involves a connection in Seattle that Delta cheerfully books at fifty minutes, a span of time in which you are expected to deplane, cross a major international airport, and reboard, ideally without dying. What no one mentions is that the gate you are sprinting toward should be regarded as a suggestion. Alaska Airlines had apparently decided that this was the day to reshuffle every gate it operated, more or less continuously, like a man rearranging deck chairs who has heard about the iceberg and made his peace with it. The line at their customer-service desk was fifty yards long — I paced it off mentally, because that is the kind of thing I do — and it was populated by people wearing the specific expression of travelers who have been to Gate B7, and then Gate B4, and then, on reflection, Gate B7 again. We got switched only twice, which by local standards made us lottery winners. Some men we fell in with had been bounced back and forth so many times they'd stopped picking up their bags between announcements.

We made it, in the manner of people making it — that brisk airport walk that isn't running, because running would mean admitting something. Our reward was seat 15A and the last leg north, and at 6:26 in the evening we stepped out into Juneau, where a complete stranger was — in theory — waiting to hand us the keys to her Volvo.

Turo calls this "peer-to-peer car sharing." I call it borrowing a car from a woman I met on the internet, and I would like the record to show that of the two of us, Georgianna was the calm one. The profile said our host was an All-Star with a 5.0 rating across 45 trips, which sounded impressive until I reflected that I have roughly the same rating at my accounting firm and I still wouldn't hand me a Volvo. I had spent portions of three separate evenings imagining how the handoff might go wrong.

Here is how it went. We walked out the main entrance of the Juneau airport — the whole airport has one, the way a house does — directly into short-term parking, where the Volvo sat waiting. Our host was not there. Our host, I suspect, has never been there. There was a lockbox; I had a code; the box yielded the keys with a little click, and the entire transaction was over in about two minutes, which is less time than I have spent at rental counters after my turn came.

You have to understand what "walking out of an airport to your car" means to someone from Atlanta. At Hartsfield-Jackson, reaching your own vehicle is a pilgrimage — an underground train, a series of escalators the height of grain silos, several irreversible decisions about which color of parking deck you believe in, and at the end of it a hike with your bags to a car that is, statistically speaking, not where you left it. In Juneau we strolled out the front door into the evening air and were driving inside two minutes, and I genuinely did not know how to feel about it. All that dread, and the stranger hadn't even bothered to exist. It remains the smoothest car acquisition of my life. About two minutes, front door to driving.

The whales were the next morning at 7:30, which meant leaving the hotel at 6:45, which meant that our first full day of vacation began earlier than most of our working ones. The drive out along Egan Drive supplied our first honest Alaska statistic: bald eagles on the light poles. Not an eagle — eagles, plural, one every second or third pole, perched with the settled air of equipment the highway department had installed and stopped maintaining. In Georgia the sighting of one bald eagle reorganizes an afternoon; people pull over, phone footage is reviewed at dinner. Here the national symbol lines the commuter route like it's waiting for a bus, and by the third mile you accept the truth of it: this is their natural habitat now, and the light poles were built in it. We drove ten miles of this to Auke Bay, bought a parking pass from a kiosk — the kiosk was not in a hurry — and presented ourselves at Statter Harbor for what we had booked months earlier as "Harv's Original 6-Passenger Whale Watch." I had chosen it precisely for the smallness. Six passengers. Intimate. Practically our own boat.

At the dock we learned we'd been moved to the eighteen-passenger boat.

I was preparing my speech — I am never not preparing a speech — when they refunded us the difference before I could open my mouth. Back on the card, unprompted, without discussion, while I was still assembling my grievance. I want to say this carefully: nobody refunds money unprompted. Entire branches of my profession exist because nobody refunds money unprompted. I nearly asked them to frame it.

The boat was run by Captain Dan and First Mate Dan, an arrangement I assume simplifies things enormously at hiring time, and the two Dans turned out to be worth any number of missing intimacies. The day was — and you must understand we had packed for sideways rain — sunny, warm, and dead calm, the water flat as a boardroom table. On the way out a group of porpoises found the bow and escorted us, surfing the pressure wave a few feet in front of the hull with the easy showing-off of locals who know the tourists are watching.

Then the whales. Not a whale — pods, plural, surfacing and blowing and rolling under that improbable sunshine while the two Dans positioned the boat and narrated like men who have never once gotten tired of this. Captain Dan informed us that a humpback puts away some fifteen tons of food a week, and that in these waters this feat is surpassed only by the passengers of cruise ships. He said this with the neutrality of a man stating a marine biological fact, which as far as I can tell is exactly what it is. Two of the ships in question sat at anchor downtown even as he spoke, digesting.

When the breaching started, a curious thing happened: a second boat appeared. Same company — they run several — and it had been dispatched, we learned, to take pictures of our boat taking pictures of the whales. This struck Captain Dan as an opportunity, and he raised his own camera and photographed them photographing us photographing the whales, at which point the humpbacks were arguably the only parties present engaged in an authentic experience. I don't know where the chain ended. Somewhere in Juneau there may be a third boat, a drone, a satellite. I have accepted that in at least one frame in this world I appear as a small pink smudge labeled tourist, documenting, and honestly, no argument. At some point we passed a buoy that had been colonized by sea lions, who were conducting what I can only describe as an ongoing eviction hearing — a dozen of them piled on a platform built for four, bellowing, shoving, and periodically knocking one another into the sea with a splash and a total absence of hard feelings. It was the best thing I saw all day, and I say that having seen humpback whales.

And then, on the way back, the glacier. From the water, the Mendenhall is a broad white river easing down between the mountains toward town, serene as a postcard. We admired it the way you admire scenery — pleasantly, ignorantly. It did not look like the sort of thing that keeps a municipal evacuation map current. Nobody mentioned, as we bobbed there taking pictures, that the Mendenhall runs a side business. We would learn about that on Wednesday.

Ledger to date: Dans, two. Gate changes, two. Boats booked, one; boats sailed, a larger one. Porpoises, uncounted. Sea lions, a dozen on a platform built for four. Faith in humanity: up. Body temperature: also up.
