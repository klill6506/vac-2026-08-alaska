Chapter 6 · history · Aug 12

The Serum Run

Every musher we met on that glacier had either run the Iditarod or intended to. It came up naturally, the way a profession does — the way accountants will tell you, unprompted, which busy season nearly finished them. And underneath all of it, never quite stated, was a thing that happened in the winter of 1925.

Nome sits on the western edge of Alaska, on the Bering Sea, about as far from anywhere as a town can arrange to be. In January of 1925 the local physician began seeing cases of diphtheria among the children there.

Diphtheria in a small isolated town in 1925 was not an illness. It was a countdown. It is brutally contagious, it kills children in particular, and the one thing that stops it is antitoxin serum, of which Nome had essentially none that was still any good.

There was serum in Anchorage. The difficulty was the eight hundred or so miles between the two, in the depths of an Arctic winter, with the port iced in and aviation in a state that could most charitably be described as experimental. Open-cockpit biplanes, in January, over that country, were not a serious proposal.

What Alaska had instead was dogs.

They arranged a relay. The serum went by train as far as the railroad went, and from there it was handed off from dog team to dog team across the interior and along the coast — twenty teams, roughly, running legs in appalling weather, in temperatures that reached somewhere in the region of sixty below, passing a twenty-pound cylinder of serum from one musher to the next like the world's most consequential baton.

The average leg was about thirty-one miles.

Then there was Leonhard Seppala.

Seppala took the longest and by common agreement the most dangerous section of the entire route, and he did it with a lead dog named Togo, who was twelve years old at the time — an age at which most sled dogs have long since been retired to lying about being admired. Between them, Seppala and Togo covered something in the order of two hundred and sixty miles. Against an average of thirty-one.

They did the part nobody else wanted, and they did roughly eight times as much of it as anybody else.

The serum reached Nome in the early hours of the second of February, 1925, carried on the final leg — about fifty-five miles of it — by a musher named Gunnar Kaasen, behind a lead dog named Balto.

The children of Nome lived. That is the part that actually matters, and it is worth saying plainly before we get to the rest.

Here is the rest.

Balto became one of the most famous animals in the world. There was a statue of him in Central Park within the year, which is still there, and which generations of New York children have been photographed sitting on. There is another in Anchorage. He was, for a while, the most celebrated dog in America.

Togo, who had run two hundred and sixty miles of the same emergency, including the stretch that could most easily have killed everybody involved, did not get Central Park.

I want to be fair to Balto, who was a working sled dog doing his job in sixty-below darkness and had no opinions whatsoever about statuary. And I want to be fair to Kaasen, who ran the last leg into Nome and got the serum through. Neither of them did anything wrong. They arrived, which is what the newspapers could see, and arriving at the end of a relay is the part that photographs.

But among the people who actually knew — the mushers, the ones who understood what those distances meant in that weather — there was never any real argument about whose run it had been.

This is the second time in this book that Alaska has handed the credit to whoever happened to be standing in the right place at the finish. Joe Juneau got a town for buying drinks in a bar, while the man who knew where the gold was got nothing at all. Forty-four years later, a dog got Manhattan for the last fifty-five miles of a two-hundred-and-sixty-mile emergency.

There is no villain in either story. That is what makes them worth telling. Nobody stole anything. It is just that credit tends to settle on whatever the public happened to be looking at, and Alaska — being new enough that the paperwork is still legible — lets you watch the process happen.

Togo has been getting his due lately, slowly, in the way these things eventually correct. He has his own statue now. There have been films. If you talk to mushers, you will find that in that community he was never lost in the first place; he simply belonged to the people who knew, rather than to the people who read the papers.

Which brings me back to a hundred-odd dogs on an icefield above Juneau, and the young men and women who live up there six days at a stretch to work with them, and who can tell you the name and the temperament of every animal in camp.

They know exactly which dogs are the good ones. They always have. That is more or less the whole tradition, and it has never had much to do with statues.

Sources: the 1925 serum run to Nome — contemporaneous accounts and subsequent histories; American Kennel Club and Cleveland Museum of Natural History material on Togo and Balto.
