Chapter 4 · history · Aug 11

The Tongass and the Bear Island

Nobody packs for a rainforest when they go to Alaska. You pack for cold. You picture ice and spruce and a great deal of sky, and you are not entirely wrong, but the corner of the state we spent our first five days in is, technically and actually, a rainforest — the largest contiguous temperate rainforest left on earth.

It is called the Tongass, and it covers 16.7 million acres, which makes it the largest national forest in the United States by a distance that stops being meaningful. It is bigger than ten American states. It is bigger than seventy-five member nations of the United Nations. Juneau sits inside it. The whale boat, the glacier, the dog camp, the bear creek — all of it happened within the boundaries of a single forest which, if you set it down in Europe, would swallow Switzerland and ask what else you had.

And within the Tongass, near its northern end, sit three islands that have between them one of the strangest concentrations of animals anywhere on the planet.

They are Admiralty, Baranof and Chichagof, and because Alaskans are a practical people who have a great many long names to get through, they are known simply as the ABC Islands.

Roughly three thousand brown bears live in that region, with the densest populations on those three islands. Admiralty alone holds about sixteen hundred of them — approaching one bear for every square mile, the highest density of brown bears anywhere in North America. The bears outnumber Admiralty's human residents by something close to three to one.

I would like to note, purely as an observation, that we flew to the middle island of the three on purpose, having paid in advance.

Chichagof is where we went. Waterfall Creek, salmon running, ten or twelve bears within a couple of hundred yards of six people in borrowed rain gear. Standing there, it felt like an extraordinary stroke of luck. It was not luck. It was arithmetic. We had been delivered to one of the few places on earth where that many large predators can reliably be found in one small area, at the one time of year when the food comes to them, and the operation that took us there does this several times a week all summer. The bears were not putting on a show for us. We had simply been dropped into the middle of their busiest month.

Now for the part I did not expect, and which has quietly delighted me ever since I went looking.

The bears on those three islands are not quite like other brown bears.

They look like brown bears. They behave like brown bears — the ones we watched were doing exactly what brown bears do in August, which is stand in cold water and eat salmon with tremendous concentration. By every ordinary measure they are brown bears.

But their mitochondrial DNA — the strand inherited only from the mother, tracing the maternal line back and back — is more closely related to polar bears than to other brown bears.

This confused people for years. One early reading of it suggested that polar bears might have descended from these island bears, which would have made Chichagof and its neighbours something like the ancestral home of the entire species. More recent work, using nuclear DNA rather than just the maternal line, has turned that on its head, and the answer it produced is better than the question.

The ABC Islands bears appear to be the descendants of polar bears. Not their ancestors — their heirs.

The picture is roughly this. At the end of the last glacial period the ice retreated, and a population of polar bears was left behind on those islands, stranded on the wrong side of a vanishing world. Brown bears are dispersers — the males in particular will swim considerable distances looking for opportunity — and over thousands of years brown bear males crossed the water and bred with the polar bear females who were already there. Generation after generation, the population became browner, and behaved more like brown bears, until eventually they simply were brown bears in everything you could observe.

But the maternal line never entirely washed out. It is still in there, carried down the female side across all those centuries, quietly recording where the mothers came from.

I have thought about this more than is reasonable.

We stood on a creek bank on Chichagof Island and watched perhaps a dozen animals fish, and every one of them was, in the oldest part of its inheritance, descended from bears that lived on the sea ice. They have no idea. They are entirely, unimprovably brown bears. They have made a complete success of it. The ice that stranded their ancestors has been gone for so long that the island grew a rainforest on top of the evidence.

There is a version of this book in which I resist drawing a line between that and the town forty miles east of there, which is named after the third man in a room of three and has been quietly misfiling the credit ever since. But the line is right there. Alaska is a place where the story on the surface is frequently not the story in the record. A town keeps the wrong name. A dog gets the wrong statue, as we will see shortly. And on three islands in the largest rainforest on earth, a population of bears goes about its business every August with somebody else's ancestry written down inside it, unread, for ten thousand years.

I find I like them more for it.

Sources: U.S. Forest Service, Tongass National Forest; Alaska Department of Fish and Game, brown bear management reports; University of California, Santa Cruz, and associated genomic research on ABC Islands bear ancestry (2013).
