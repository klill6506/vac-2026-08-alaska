Chapter 12 · history · Aug 15

The Bridge You Cannot Cross

There is a single road into Denali National Park. One road, ninety-two miles long, one lane wide, and gravel for most of its length — which, for a park larger than the state of New Hampshire, is a startlingly modest piece of infrastructure. Everything the ordinary visitor sees of six million acres, they see from that road or from a bus on it.

Our bus went forty-three miles up it and then turned around.

I had known this in advance, in the flat way you know a fact you have read in a confirmation email. The road is closed at Mile 43. There is a landslide. Fine. It was only afterward, when I went looking, that I understood what was actually sitting out there past the turnaround, and I have thought about it more than almost anything else on the trip.

The place is called Pretty Rocks, which is the kind of name given by people who have not yet had any trouble from something. It sits at Mile 45.4, at Polychrome Pass, and it has been creeping downhill for as long as anyone has been watching. For decades this was a maintenance item rather than a crisis. A few inches a year. You bring in gravel, you regrade the road, you go home.

Then, sometime after 2014, it began to accelerate.

The reason is worth stating plainly, because it explains a great deal about Alaska that has nothing to do with roads. The slope at Pretty Rocks is held together by ice. Not built on ice — held together by it, the way mortar holds a wall. Ice-rich permafrost had frozen that hillside into something road-shaped and stable, and permafrost, as the name rather confidently promises, was permanent.

It is not. As the ground warms, the ice inside it goes, and what is left is not soil. It is slurry. The hillside stops being a hillside and becomes something closer to a very slow liquid, and the road laid across it becomes a thing written on the surface of a river.

The numbers got away from the park quickly. What had been inches a year became inches a day, and the road crews found themselves in one of those situations that would be funny if it were not so expensive — hauling gravel up a mountain to fill a hole that was widening faster than they could fill it. By 2021 it was over. You cannot maintain a road that is leaving.

Now, what do you do?

The park's answer was the one I find genuinely admirable, in the way that engineering occasionally is: if the ground will not hold still, stop asking it to. Do not rebuild the road across the slide. Span it. Put a bridge over the whole moving hillside, anchored at each end in something that isn't going anywhere, and let the slurry travel underneath.

So they built one. It is not a small thing — a long-span bridge, in a national park, at Polychrome Pass, reachable only by that same single-lane gravel road, in a place with a construction season measured in weeks. The money came from three separate federal sources, including disaster relief, and the overall fix has been reported at around a hundred million dollars. In the autumn of 2025 the engineers "launched" it — pushed the span out across the void, which is a procedure I have now watched several videos of and recommend to anyone.

By the summer we visited, that bridge was essentially finished.

It was sitting there, a couple of miles past the point where our driver turned the bus around. A hundred-million-dollar solution to a sixty-year-old problem, complete, spanning the landslide it was designed to defeat — and not open.

I want to be fair about why, because it is not incompetence; it is sequence. Getting the bridge in was the hard part, but it was not the only part. The road west of the bridge still had to be rebuilt, and until that is done there is nowhere for a bus to go once it crosses. A bridge that delivers you to eighty miles of unrepaired gravel is not a route. So the road stays shut at Mile 43 for the season, and full service to Eielson and Wonder Lake and Kantishna waits for the following year.

Which means that in the summer of 2026, an entire national park's worth of visitors drove to Mile 43, looked at a mountain, and turned around two miles short of a finished bridge that none of them was allowed to cross.

I find this magnificent. Not the delay — the shape of it. Somebody identified an enormous problem, engineered a genuinely elegant answer to it, hauled that answer up a gravel road into the subarctic, installed it successfully, and then had to leave it there, complete and idle, for a year, because the next part wasn't ready. Every large organization I have ever billed hours to has done a version of this. It is oddly comforting to learn that the National Park Service does it too, only with better scenery.

And there is one more thing, which is the part I keep turning over.

The bridge is not a repair. Nobody thinks the hillside is going to stop. The bridge is an admission — a very expensive, very well-made admission that the ground itself has changed its terms, and that the best available answer is not to fix the ground but to stop touching it. They are not restoring the road. They are getting out of its way.

We rode forty-three miles into the largest wilderness I have ever seen, and the most interesting thing out there was a piece of civil engineering I never laid eyes on, built because the word permafrost has quietly stopped being true.
