Chapter 2 · history · Aug 9–10

A Town Named for the Wrong Man

Every place is named after somebody, and the somebody is hardly ever the person who found it. This is one of the quiet consistencies of human settlement, and Alaska — being younger than most places and therefore less practiced at covering its tracks — offers an unusually clear view of how the thing is done.

In the summer of 1880, two prospectors named Joe Juneau and Richard Harris were poking around the panhandle of southeast Alaska in the manner of prospectors everywhere, which is to say hopefully and without much idea. They were not finding gold. What changed their fortunes was a Tlingit leader named Kowee, who knew the country the way you know your own kitchen, and who took them up a drainage that would come to be called Gold Creek, and then — when the creek proved merely encouraging rather than spectacular — beyond it, into a bowl in the mountains now known as Silver Bow Basin.

There they found it. Not colors in a pan, not a promising trace, but the first major gold strike in Alaska, in quantities sufficient to summon several thousand men to a shoreline that had, the previous year, contained none of them.

Three men are involved in this story, and one of them knew where the gold was. It is worth watching how the credit gets distributed, because the paperwork rarely follows the labor.

Harris and Juneau staked a townsite at the mouth of the creek and named it, with the modesty that has always characterized men who have just become rich, Harrisburg. After Harris. Who had been standing next to the man who knew where the gold was.

This did not hold. The settlement was renamed Rockwell, briefly, after a naval officer — the nineteenth century being an era in which any sufficiently nearby lieutenant might get a town — and then, on the fourteenth of December 1881, the miners gathered to settle the question by vote.

Seventy-two ballots were cast. Juneau took forty-seven. Harrisburg managed twenty-one. Rockwell, poor Rockwell, received four.

And here we arrive at the detail that makes the whole business worth retelling, because the record suggests that Joe Juneau secured this result by the simple expedient of buying drinks for the electorate. I want to be careful here: this is reported rather than proven, in the way that most of the good parts of history are reported rather than proven. But it has the ring of a true thing. Forty-seven men out of seventy-two, in a mining camp, in December, in southeast Alaska, where it rains with real commitment — you would not need to buy very good drinks.

So the town is named Juneau. Not for the man who found the gold, who was Kowee, and who has no town. Not for the partner who was there the whole time and got his name on it first, who was Harris, and who lost it. But for the third man, who understood the one thing neither of the others did, which is that history is not awarded to the deserving but to whoever is still buying at closing time.

It is worth following Joe Juneau a little further, because his subsequent career does something rather satisfying to the moral of the story.

He did not get rich. Or rather he got rich repeatedly and briefly, in the manner of a man who spends gold roughly as fast as it can be dug up, which by all accounts is what he did. The strike that founded a city and produced decades of industrial mining did not produce a comfortable old age for Joe Juneau. In the late 1890s, well past sixty, he went north again for the Klondike — the last gold rush, the one that ate people — and there, in Dawson, he ended up running a small restaurant. In March of 1899 he died of pneumonia. A city bore his name. He was operating a diner.

But the citizens of Juneau, to their credit, did not forget the man whose drinks they had accepted. In 1903 they brought his body home, and buried it in Evergreen Cemetery, where Richard Harris also lies — the two of them together again, in a town with one of their names on it.

The burial took place on the sixteenth of August. Which means nothing at all, except that I am writing this because I spent my sixtieth birthday, one hundred and twenty-three years later, standing on a glacier about three hundred miles north of his grave. History is full of these alignments. They signify nothing. I have noted it anyway, because noticing things that signify nothing is, as I believe I have mentioned, essentially my entire personality.

There is a coda to Juneau's naming problem, and it is the best part.

Juneau is the capital of Alaska. It is also the only state capital in the United States that you cannot drive to. There is no road. There has never been a road. To reach the seat of government of the largest state in the union you must fly, or you must take a ferry, and the ferry is a genuinely lovely way to travel if you have three days and no particular commitments.

Alaskans have found this arrangement irritating for a very long time, and being Americans, they have voted about it. Repeatedly.

In 1960 they voted on moving the capital toward Cook Inlet, and fifty-six percent said no. In 1962 they voted on moving it to within thirty miles of Anchorage, and fifty-five percent said no. Then, in 1974, they changed their minds and voted yes — the capital would leave the southeast and relocate somewhere on the road system, like a normal capital in a normal place. In 1976 they went further and chose the spot: a place called Willow.

And then somebody worked out what it would cost.

In 1978 the voters were asked to approve a bond issue of nine hundred and sixty-six million dollars to build the new capital they had already voted for and already located. Seventy-four percent of them said no. A further attempt in 1982 also failed, by which point the estimated cost had climbed past two and a half billion.

So Alaska voted to move its capital, chose where to move it to, and then declined, twice and decisively, to pay for any of it. Willow remains a place where nothing in particular happened. Juneau remains the capital, unreachable by road, governing a state that has formally resolved to stop doing so and then thought better of the invoice.

Which is, when you think about it, exactly how the town got its name in the first place. A vote is cheap. Following through is expensive. Joe Juneau understood this in 1881, in a bar, with a tab he could afford — and it has been working out for him ever since.
