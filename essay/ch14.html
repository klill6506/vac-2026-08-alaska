Chapter 14 · history · Aug 16

Landing Airplanes on Glaciers

There is a bowl of ice high in the Alaska Range, ringed by granite walls, that is officially called the Don Sheldon Amphitheater.

I did not get to stand in it. We flew toward it on my sixtieth birthday and the weather closed every door as we reached it, and Colin — correctly — declined to put an aircraft down on a surface he couldn't quite see. So I have no first-hand account of the place.

What I do have is the story of the man it's named after, which turns out to be the story of how anybody came to be landing aeroplanes on glaciers at all.

Don Sheldon flew twenty-six combat missions over Europe as a tail gunner in a B-17, which is a job with actuarial characteristics I would rather not dwell on. He had learned to fly through a government training program before the war. Afterwards he bought a surplus military aircraft and took it north to Talkeetna, a small settlement at the foot of the Alaska Range, where he and a partner named Stub Morrison started an air service.

And there he worked out, largely by doing it, how to land a small aeroplane on a glacier.

I want to convey what that involves, because "glacier landing" sounds like a category of thing that already existed and Sheldon simply took up. It did not. A glacier is not a runway. It is a moving surface with an uphill slope, no usable depth perception in flat light, crevasses that open where they like, and snow conditions that change hour to hour. You cannot go around for a second attempt on most of them — the walls are in the way. You commit, and the landing either works or it becomes an entirely different kind of event.

Sheldon learned to read that. He also learned to read the weather in the Alaska Range, which is among the least cooperative weather on the continent, and he became the man they called when climbers on Denali got into trouble. He flew rescues into places nobody else would attempt, repeatedly, for decades.

In 1955 he did something that delights me.

He filed a claim, under the Homestead Act, on 4.9 acres of rock in the middle of the Ruth Glacier's amphitheater. The Homestead Act — the same instrument that put farmers on the Great Plains — used to put a bush pilot on a nunatak in the Alaska Range, surrounded on all sides by ice.

He then ferried building materials up there, load by load, in a small aeroplane, and built a hexagonal cabin fourteen feet across. It is known as the Mountain House. It is still there. You can still rent it.

And his actual plan was larger. He intended to build a proper lodge and install a rope tow, and run the amphitheater as a ski destination — an idea so magnificently premature that I had to read it twice. A ski resort, in the nineteen-fifties, on a glacier reachable only by an aeroplane flown by essentially one man.

He never built it. He was diagnosed with cancer and died in 1975, before the lodge or the rope tow existed. In 1980 the national park expanded to take in the Ruth Glacier, and his family's land was grandfathered in, and the rules of the place changed around the little hexagonal house he had flown up there piece by piece.

There is a thread running through this book about Alaska handing credit to the wrong party. A town named for the man who bought the drinks rather than the man who found the gold. A dog with a statue in Central Park for the last fifty-five miles of somebody else's two hundred and sixty.

This is the one where they got it right.

The bowl is named the Don Sheldon Amphitheater. Not for a senator, not for a donor, not for whoever was standing nearby when the photographs were taken — for the pilot who worked out how to get in there, and who kept going back, and who thought the view was worth putting a house on.

Every flightseeing operation running out of Talkeetna and Healy today, including the one that took eight of us up on my birthday, is working in a trade he largely invented. Colin's whole profession — reading Alaska Range weather from a cockpit and deciding what can and cannot be attempted today — is Sheldon's legacy about as directly as a legacy gets.

Which puts a slightly different colour on our morning.

We flew out to the mountains, and the weather beat us, and the pilot turned us around rather than gamble on ice he couldn't see. I have been mildly disappointed about that for two days. But it seems to me now that declining a landing you can't see is not a failure of the tradition Sheldon started. It's the tradition working exactly as intended, by a man who intends to be flying next week.

The amphitheater keeps. It has been up there a long time, and it is named after somebody who understood better than anyone that the mountain decides.

Sources: Sheldon Air Service historical materials; National Park Service, Denali National Park & Preserve; contemporary profiles of Don Sheldon and accounts of the Mountain House and its Homestead Act claim.
